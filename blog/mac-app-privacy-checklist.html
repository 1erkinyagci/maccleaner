How to Vet a Mac App Before You Install It (2026 Checklist)

MacCleaner · 2026-08-05 · 6 min read

Short answer

Before installing a Mac app in 2026, check five things: it is signed and notarized by Apple (no Gatekeeper bypass), it has a real website and a privacy policy, it explains why it needs each permission, it does not send your data anywhere without saying so, and it can be fully uninstalled. Apps with no site, no policy and vague permissions are the ones to avoid.

Why this matters more in 2026

It has never been easier to ship a Mac app — which means more of them arrive with no website, no privacy policy and no accountability. A storage cleaner asks for deep access to your files, so it deserves more scrutiny than most.

The five-point checklist

  • Signed & notarized — it opens without Gatekeeper workarounds. If instructions say "right-click → Open to bypass", ask why.
  • Real website + privacy policy — a plain-language statement of what leaves your Mac.
  • Explained permissions — Full Disk Access is legitimate for a cleaner (macOS blocks reading ~/.Trash without it), but the app should say why and still work honestly if you decline.
  • No hidden telemetry — local-first should mean local-first.
  • Reversible actions — deletions go somewhere recoverable first.

Applying it to cleaners specifically

Cleaners add two more: read-only scanning (finding is separate from deleting) and review before delete (you can see every path). Anything that inflates numbers or auto-selects your personal files fails. Longer version: is Mac cleaning software safe?

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if a Mac app is safe to install?

Check that it is signed and notarized by Apple, has a real website and privacy policy, explains each permission it requests, doesn't send data without saying so, and can be uninstalled cleanly.

Is it safe to give an app Full Disk Access?

It's a legitimate request for backup and cleaner apps, because macOS blocks reading protected locations like ~/.Trash without it. What matters is that the app explains why and still behaves honestly if you decline.

Why do some Mac apps need you to bypass Gatekeeper?

Because they aren't notarized by Apple. That's a red flag for anything touching your files — prefer notarized apps that open normally.
